The gap between what AI can do and what actually works

AI · HEALTH

Stanford-Harvard audit of clinical AI finds a 'deployment gap' that algorithms alone cannot close

The first State of Clinical AI report, from the Stanford-Harvard ARISE network, synthesises hundreds of studies and delivers a clear verdict: AI performs well in controlled trials but breaks down when it meets real hospital workflows.

The ARISE report distinguishes carefully between benchmark performance and real-world utility. Prediction models that flag deteriorating patients or assist radiologists reading mammograms show the strongest results, but even these tools see adoption collapse to near-zero in busy ICUs and emergency departments if they require physicians to leave their normal EHR workflow — even briefly. The authors call this the 'deployment gap' and argue it is now the field's central unsolved problem.

A recurring finding is over-reliance: clinicians in multiple studies followed incorrect model recommendations even when the errors were detectable. The report also flags 'brittleness' — models that perform at superhuman levels on benchmark tasks but cannot reliably flag their own uncertainty in edge cases. These failure modes are qualitatively different from simple low accuracy and are harder to catch in pre-deployment validation.

The report's headline conclusion is that the era of simply building smarter algorithms is over. Future clinical AI must be evaluated on seamless integration with medical workflows, not just accuracy scores. The authors recommend that hospitals track AI usage rates in production — not just model performance — as the primary adoption metric going forward.

Art of the Day
№ 02
Trompe-l’Œil Still Life with a Flower Garland and a Curtain
Adriaen van der Spelt & Frans van Mieris · 1658
Two Dutch masters collaborated on this small panel, each contributing what they did best: van der Spelt painted the flower garland, van Mieris the stone cartouche behind it. The curtain — blue-grey silk painted across one corner of the composition — is pure fiction, a piece of cloth that exists only in paint but is rendered with enough weight and fold to make you want to pull it aside. The panel won a prize from the Leiden painters’ guild the year it was finished, suggesting contemporaries already understood the wit: a painting of a curtain hiding a painting.

Bhairavi is the raga of parting — traditionally the last raga played at a concert, carrying a quality of tender finality. Ravi Shankar's live rendition from the Royal Festival Hall is unhurried, the sitar tracing each phrase with the kind of care that only happens when a musician knows the morning will ask something of you. № 07
The Palace of Illusions
Chitra Banerjee Divakaruni

A retelling of the Mahabharata entirely through Draupadi's eyes — a character the original epic treats as catalyst rather than subject. Divakaruni forces a reckoning with how canonical stories are shaped by whose voice is centred: the war, the dharma debates, the dice game all look different when the person most wronged by them is the narrator. For a reader who enjoyed Kaikeyi and is drawn to Indian mythology retellings, this is the natural companion — it asks the same question Sudha Murty and Kavya Mandana ask, but with sharper political edge.

A Thought for Today
№ 08
பெண்கள் ஆண்களுக்கு அடிமைகளாக இருக்கக் கூடாது. அவர்கள் சுயமரியாதையுடன் சுதந்திரமாக வாழ வேண்டும். பெண்கள் அடிமைத்தனத்திலிருந்து விடுபட்டால்தான் சமூகம் முன்னேறும்.
“Women should not be slaves to men. They should have self-respect and should be able to live independently. Only when women are free from subordination will society progress.”
— Periyar · Drawn from Periyar's speeches and writings in the Kudi Arasu journal and Self-Respect Movement publications, 1920s–1930s; the argument runs throughout works such as 'Penn Yean Adimaiyanal' (Why Woman Became a Slave).
Periyar argued that the subjugation of women is not natural or inevitable — it was engineered through religion, custom, and law to keep women economically dependent and socially invisible. He insisted that a person who cannot own their own life cannot truly think freely, and that any movement for human dignity that excluded half the population was building on sand.
At a time when women's autonomy — over their bodies, labour, and choices — remains contested in law and daily life, Periyar's framing is pointed: women's freedom is not a secondary cause to be addressed after other struggles; it is the measure of whether a society has progressed at all.

Trail of the Day
№ 09
Rupin Pass Trek
Uttarakhand–Himachal Pradesh, India
52 km
Distance
+1800 m
Elevation
7 days
Duration
“Standing on the snowfield below the pass, the Rupin waterfall tumbles down a cliff face directly opposite you, framed by two hanging valleys — it looks like a scene invented by someone who had never been forced to be plausible.”
The Rupin valley was a grazing corridor long before it was a trekking destination — Gujjar and Kinnauri shepherds moved livestock over this pass between the warmer Uttarakhand foothills and the high Kinnaur pastures each season, a rhythm that still continues alongside the trekking groups. The trek's centrepiece, the hanging valley with the waterfall visible from across the snowfield, sits at around 13,000 ft and was largely unknown outside regional shepherds until India Hikes began running it in the early 2010s; within a few years it had become the single most photographed frame in Indian recreational trekking.
Late May is exactly the window when the Rupin Pass route sheds its most dangerous winter ice but still holds deep snowfields — the combination that makes the crossing both feasible and spectacular. If you are planning any high-altitude Himalayan trek for early summer, this is the one to book right now before the June batches fill. The route crosses from Uttarakhand into Himachal in a single long pass day, and that geographic punctuation is part of the point.
  • Base access: fly or drive to Dehradun, then drive to Dhaula village (Uttarkashi district) — roughly 8–9 hours from Dehradun; the trail begins here
  • The trek crosses into Himachal Pradesh on pass day and exits at Sangla in Kinnaur, so carry ID; arrange return transport from Sangla (Rampur–Shimla highway) in advance
  • Snow on the upper reaches is significant in late May — microspikes or crampons and trekking poles are essential, not optional; book with a registered operator (India Hikes runs fixed departures)
Best season: May–June, September–October
